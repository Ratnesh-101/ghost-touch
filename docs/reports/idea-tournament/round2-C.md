## 1. Three strongest attacks on Ghost Walk

**A. The headline claim contradicts the pitch's own rules.** Step 3 says the ghost moves right at a constant x speed, so the whole curve takes 10 s. Step 6 then says that on the flat part "your hand **stops**". It cannot do both. With x running at a constant speed, the hand on the flat part keeps sliding right at the same speed. Only the *vertical* motion stops. The motion-sensor graph-matching lab D cites is one-dimensional body motion: the walker's distance from the sensor *is* the y value. On a phone, Ghost Walk mixes the y signal with a constant sideways drift. So "her hand just performed the graph" and "the derivative, felt in the shoulder" are overstated. What the student actually feels is the curve's shape traced at a set pace, which is close to what Explore plus graph scan already gives. The citation ("Thornton and Sokoloff, 1990s") does not appear anywhere in the repo or `docs/research/`, so it rests on memory and cannot be checked.

**B. Two machines would drive the same motor and tone.** In Explore the engine already buzzes `vibrate(80)` whenever the finger is on a line, and plays the theremin at the *finger's* height on any polyline (`core/hapticEngine.ts:181`). It is pumped by the 60 ms hold keep-alive (`flow/explore.ts:713`, `:745`). Ghost Walk adds a 50 ms `flow/chase.ts` ticker that gives the *same* buzz a new meaning ("you are holding the ghost"), and the *same* theremin a new meaning (the ghost's height while you are lost). That breaks Requirement 39 ("one sound, one meaning") and the report's theme 1, "many voices, no conductor". If the finger is on the curve 40 px behind the ghost, the engine says "line" while chase says "lost". So chase has to mute the engine. That means a third set of guards in `Explore.tsx`, like the Guide guards of Requirement 38.4, and two timers fighting over one finger. This exact seam produced I01, the biggest P0 bug in the improvement report (`fable-project-improvement-report.md:82`). D calls it "one pointer branch". The repo's history says otherwise.

**C. It covers a narrow slice of content, puts heavy load on an eyes-closed user, and shows a score nobody has validated.** `curveYAtX` returns null unless `kind === 'function_graph'` (`core/curve.ts:27`). So Chase is absent from 2 of the 3 shipped samples, and D adds a fourth sample just to have something to demo. For real photos, a straight-piece distance-time graph may come back as segments rather than a polyline, and Chase only walks polylines (`isWalkable` needs one). The task itself is hard eyes-closed: follow a target that keeps moving, inside a 22 px radius, with an on/off buzz as the only signal while you hold it, and no direction word until 1.5 s after you lose it. Guide already steers a finger with pitch, pan and spoken direction, and already has a "peak" target (`guide/semantic.ts:93`). So the steering part overlaps an existing feature. Finally, "You stayed with the ghost 84 percent" is a spoken grade. It mostly measures time spent waiting for a patient ghost, it has never been tried on a person, and it is the kind of in-class surprise Amara's persona asks us to delete (`fable-persona-amara-blind-student.md:36`).

## 2. Attacks I expect from D, and my answers

| D's likely attack | Answer |
|---|---|
| "Ghostwriter already adds points and edges" | It uses the same pure functions on purpose (`core/correct.ts:284`, `:310`). But Ghostwriter is a *visual* editor that the app deliberately hides from students: Requirement 48 offers Correct only with Teacher tools, so a blind student never lands in a screen she cannot see. ## 3. One improvement: the "rise and run" readout
Pins sit on integer grid crossings, so slopes can be computed *exactly*, with no AI and no measuring error. When the finger rests on a stitched segment, it speaks "up 2, right 1". After "join in order" the summary says "every step goes up 2 for 1 across" or "steps differ". This is pure arithmetic in `describeWork` (Kiro property: the readout matches the integer differences of the pins), and it answers the homework question "Is it a straight line?" from the student's own data. This also takes D's best idea, a felt and spoken sense of rate of change, and does it more cheaply and more honestly: the student builds the rate herself, and nothing moves under her finger. A teacher worksheet can switch the readout off for tests, so it never does the exam for her.
